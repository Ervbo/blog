What is a Scribe?

I've been experimenting with talking to AI in conversational, dialogue mode instead of typing — a new revision of Copilot makes it feel much more natural, though I still can't pause and think mid-sentence without it jumping in to answer whatever I'd said up to that point. What follows was completely unprepared — I didn't script a single question, and I actually started out asking about quantum mechanics before changing my mind and landing on the book of Ezra instead. If any of you want a demo of how this works, just let me know.

I asked it to stick strictly to what's revealed in the Bible text itself rather than pulling in outside commentaries, and started in Ezra chapter 7.

The detail that caught me first was verse 8 — Ezra traveling all the way from Babylon to Jerusalem. That's roughly 900 miles, mostly on foot or by caravan, and it likely took him several months to complete. Verse 6 calls him “a scribe well versed in the Law of Moses,” and I wanted to know what that actually meant practically — did they have scrolls to study from back then, and could you even borrow one?

They did have scrolls — handwritten copies of the sacred texts, carefully preserved and studied, though nothing like a modern library system. Being a scribe wasn't merely copying one scroll into another accurately. It required real understanding, interpretation, and the ability to teach the law to others. Scribes were respected as scholars, and most of them stayed anonymous in the background — we know a handful of names, like Baruch (Jeremiah's scribe) and Tertius (who physically wrote down Romans for Paul), but most are lost to history even though their work in preserving and transmitting Scripture was significant.

What surprised me is that Ezra was both a scribe and a priest — an unusual, though not unheard of, combination. Being a scribe required deep scriptural knowledge; being a priest required a specific lineage and temple duties, largely what's laid out in Leviticus — holiness, sacrifices, worship guidelines, atonement through offerings. Holding both roles gave Ezra a rare kind of influence over both the religious and civil life of Israel.

Ezra 7:9–10 explains why “the gracious hand of God” was on him: because he had determined to study and obey the law of the Lord. That's not a vague statement about divine favor — it's specifically tied to his own commitment to know and live out what he taught.

Then there's the strange detail in verse 12, where Artaxerxes calls himself “the king of kings” — a title I always assumed belonged to God alone. It turns out several ancient rulers used that title to claim dominance over lesser kings, distinct from and lesser than the biblical sense in which God alone is the ultimate sovereign. And in verse 22, Artaxerxes grants an unlimited supply of salt for the temple — salt symbolized purity and preservation in ancient ritual, was part of grain offerings and incense, and represented the enduring covenant between God and Israel.

It's remarkable, honestly, how much a pagan king seems to know and accommodate about Jewish religious practice — specifying bulls, rams, and grain offerings, advising Ezra on appointing judges (vv. 25–27). The most likely explanation is pure strategic self-interest: verse 23 has Artaxerxes reasoning that he doesn't want to “risk bringing God's anger against the realm of the king and his sons.” Political stability first, genuine reverence maybe second — and either way, Ezra gives all the credit to God's providence in verses 27–28, praising “the Lord, the God of our ancestors, who made the king want to beautify the temple.”

Chapter 8 kept the same thread going. At the Ahava Canal — a gathering point in Babylon, not a stop along the way — Ezra realized not a single Levite had volunteered for the journey, which distressed him enough to recruit about 18 more. Rather than ask the king for a military escort, he called the whole group to fast and humble themselves in prayer, specifically because he was ashamed to ask Artaxerxes for soldiers when he could ask God for protection instead (v. 22). The journey took about four months, and they arrived safely — which Ezra treats as a direct answer to that prayer, not luck.

Before setting out, Ezra weighed and documented every bit of gold and silver by number and weight, and told the priests guarding it, “you and these treasures have been set apart as holy to the Lord” — both the treasure and the people carrying it were holy, not just the metal. That level of accounting wasn't paranoia; it was transparency and stewardship, meant to preserve trust among the people once everything arrived. And arrive it did — straight to sacrifices on the newly restored altar in Jerusalem, since the temple itself was still in ruins. Rebuilding that altar first, before anything else, was the whole point: a return to their covenant relationship with God before a single stone of the temple went back up.
