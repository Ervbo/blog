Only One Way?

Have you ever been in a situation where you needed to do something or go somewhere and there was no other way? If so, try and remember the situation right now. Perhaps you needed to pass a certain class to graduate. Or you needed surgery to cure a health problem. Or you were stuck in a sewer pipe underground and the only way out was forward. Or you were standing on top of Half Dome, and a fear of heights came upon you, and there was only one way back down. I have been in all these situations. The first thing I recall about each of these examples is: I did not like it. I did not like what I had to do, and I did not like that there were no other options. No Plan B. Do or die, if you will. Fortunately, I don’t think we have many of these times in life, where our back is against the wall and there is no one and nothing that can help us.

Looking back on it now, I see the other side much more clearly. There was a way to pass the class. There was a way to get the surgery. There was a way out of the sewer pipe. There was a way down the cables. I can be thankful for that at least. In all 4 of these cases, I did what I had to do. I went the only way I could. Studying for my final exam in Physics was hard. The exam was hard. I did not ace it, but I passed it. I graduated with a degree in Mechanical Engineering. Getting through my brain surgery was hard. I was not expected to live through it. 13+ hours of surgery and 30 days in the hospital. Blind in my left eye, 50% of my frontal lobe destroyed, and unable to walk. All of it was hard. But I am alive right now. Crawling in a pitch-black sewer pipe ~2 feet in diameter for 60 yards, not knowing where or what it would lead to, was nothing short of terrifying. But I did it when I was ~5 years old and lived through it. Climbing back down Half Dome was scary, but I went down facing the rock, slowly. At the steepest part I wanted to stop, but I knew if I did I probably would not get going again, and I pushed through it.

If you would have told me at the beginning of those situations — don’t worry! There is a way through this. Be thankful for that! — I am not sure I would have even heard what you said. What is clear as day now was dark as night then.

I think much of what I described here — and add your own recollections of your similar scenarios — applies to the difficulty people have with Jesus’ statement in John 14:6:

“I am the way and the truth and the life. No one can come to the Father except through Me.”

I have talked to many people about this in my life, mostly students, and they want to hear this instead:

“I am a way and a truth and a life. Many come to the Father through Me.”

What do you think? Would it have been better if Jesus had said that (if it were true)? Would you have preferred to hear that instead?

We live in a country, a society, that values freedom. As a result, there is intense social pressure to resist the idea of there being only one way about anything. I recall some years back when ethnomathematics — the study of mathematics as practiced by different cultural groups — was in the news. It had reached the Departments of Education in both Oregon and California, which were concerned about how teachers decide whose mathematical thinking receives recognition. Which is a fair concern, but it led to some outlandish statements from its proponents: “The concept of mathematics being purely objective is unequivocally false, and teaching it is even much less so.” And: “Upholding the idea that there are always right and wrong answers perpetuate objectivity as well as fear of open conflict.” From what I recall, everything eventually settled down, and Math is still purely objective and there are always right and wrong answers. I believe the focus shifted more to the manner of instruction.

What I want to point out here is only the fact that this was the first time in my life I heard this ‘churn’ in my social circles and on the evening news — is Math objective? Is there only a right and wrong answer? Is there just one way to solve a Math problem? As an Engineer, it was as if the whole foundation of my life was under attack.

Let me pause here. Are we good that there are many situations in life that have only one solution? One way through a problem? Perhaps only one way of seeing something? Are we good that it is not necessarily a bad thing that there is only one way? How about this: the more important something is, the more it usually requires just one way, or a limited number. Finally, is it always intolerance that insists on there being one way or one solution? When is it good to be intolerant? When is it bad? Let’s address that question and be done: what should we tolerate, for what reasons, and within what limits? (What follows is adapted from a conversation I had with AI on exactly this question.)

Tolerance is allowing people to hold beliefs or live in ways you disagree with, without unjustly interfering with their freedom or mistreating them. It is generally good when it protects peaceful differences and equal rights; it becomes bad when it serves as an excuse to permit abuse, coercion, or serious injustice. Crucially, tolerating something does not necessarily mean approving of it — a point both the philosophers and UNESCO’s Declaration of Principles on Tolerance insist on.

1. What does tolerance mean?

There are two important uses of the word. Philosophical toleration: you object to a belief or practice but have stronger reasons not to suppress it — freedom of conscience, respect for other people, peaceful coexistence. (If you approve of it, ‘acceptance’ or ‘affirmation’ is the more precise word than ‘toleration.’) Broader social tolerance: respecting human diversity and other people’s right to live peacefully — while, as UNESCO says explicitly, tolerance does not require abandoning your convictions or tolerating social injustice.

Consider a religious example: “I believe your religious teaching is mistaken, but I defend your right to worship and explain your beliefs.” That is tolerance, not agreement. The philosophical point is that a reason to disagree is not automatically a sufficient reason to prohibit. This also distinguishes tolerance from moral relativism: you can believe that some ideas are objectively wrong while concluding that people should remain free to express them.

2. When is tolerance good — and when is it bad?

When it is good
Tolerance is valuable when it allows people with different convictions to coexist without requiring everyone to adopt the same worldview. Its strongest justifications are freedom of conscience, equal standing, and the recognition that ethical disagreement does not always justify coercion. Examples: allowing religious and nonreligious people to explain their beliefs; allowing political disagreement rather than silencing opponents; respecting cultural differences that do not violate others’ rights; treating people fairly even when you disapprove of their choices.

When it is bad
Tolerance becomes misplaced when ‘putting up with it’ means allowing violations of other people’s rights. UNESCO expressly rejects using tolerance to justify infringements of fundamental rights or to excuse social injustice. Overlooking violence or exploitation is not the same thing as respecting diversity. The relevant distinction is between allowing someone to live differently and allowing someone to injure or dominate someone else. And rejecting a practice does not settle how to respond: criticism, personal boundaries, institutional rules, and criminal punishment are different responses requiring different justifications.

We should also distinguish three levels: legal tolerance (should government prohibit or punish it?), social tolerance (should people be able to participate in society without unjust exclusion or harassment?), and moral approval (do I believe it is right or good?). These are not interchangeable. Something can be legally permitted yet morally criticized. Conversely, opposition to a belief does not automatically justify excluding its believers from public life.

4. Why is society tolerant of homosexuality but not murder?

The principal distinction in a liberal, rights-based framework is between consensual adult relationships and conduct that violates another person’s fundamental rights — not simply between something society likes and something it dislikes. This reflects the ‘harm principle’ associated with John Stuart Mill: coercion needs justification based on preventing harm to others, rather than merely enforcing a preferred way of life. Several qualifications matter.

Sexual orientation and murder are different categories
‘Homosexuality’ can refer to a sexual orientation, whereas murder refers to an act of unlawful killing — comparing an orientation with a violent act mixes categories. The more precise comparison is between consensual same-sex relationships among adults and the act of murder. Human-rights arguments defend people against discrimination and defend private, consensual relationships on grounds of privacy and equal treatment; they do not provide an exemption for violence, exploitation, or other rights-violating conduct.

Consent and rights explain the distinction
In a consensual adult relationship, the relationship’s same-sex character does not itself establish that someone’s rights have been violated. Murder, by contrast, destroys another person’s life. Under a harm-and-rights framework these are fundamentally different grounds for legal treatment. The same underlying standard applies regardless of orientation: consent, protection against coercion, and respect for other people’s rights.

Moral disagreement is not the same as a case for criminal punishment
Someone may believe, on religious or other moral grounds, that same-sex sexual conduct is wrong. That belief answers a moral question. Whether the government should punish it is a further question requiring an argument about the legitimate limits of law. The harm-principle tradition rejects moral disapproval by itself as sufficient grounds for coercion — the public rationale is not ‘everyone agrees this is morally right,’ but ‘disagreement alone does not justify depriving consenting adults of liberty and equal protection.’ (Consent is important, but it is not a complete ethical test for every situation; even Mill’s harm principle is more complicated than ‘anything consensual is automatically acceptable.’)

‘Society’ is not unanimous
This distinction is made in many rights-based societies, but it is not universal — some countries still criminalize consensual same-sex relations, and moral views differ within societies. So the more precise question is: why do liberal, rights-based societies distinguish consensual relationships from murder?

5. A useful test for the limits of tolerance

We can organize our thinking around five questions: 1) What exactly is being evaluated — a person, belief, expression, or action? 2) What is the objection — personal dislike, moral conviction, or an identifiable violation of rights? 3) Who is affected, and is there genuine consent? 4) What response is proposed — criticism, a boundary, exclusion, or legal punishment? 5) Is that response proportionate, and could it cause greater injustice than it prevents?

6. A possible thesis

Tolerance is neither approval of every belief nor permission for every action. It is a principled willingness to protect people’s freedom and dignity despite disagreement, while placing justified limits on conduct that violates the rights of others. That gives us a consistent standard to examine both sides: intolerance can suppress peaceful differences, while misplaced tolerance can permit injustice. The challenge is to defend the limits with reasons, rather than simply labeling whatever we like ‘tolerable’ and whatever we dislike ‘intolerable.’

Let’s stop here for now. In a future post I will come back with case studies that will help us sort this all out in regard to this question about Christ.
