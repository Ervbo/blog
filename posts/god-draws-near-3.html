God Draws Near. Part 3.

This is Part 3 — the finale — of God Draws Near. Start with Part 1 and Part 2.

Let’s pick up with God leading Israel to the Promised Land. As we saw in Part 2, God said that because Israel was stiff-necked and sinful, He would no longer go with them on the way to the Promised Land He had prepared. However, because of Moses’ intercessory prayer for his people — “If your Presence does not go with us, do not send us up from here” (Exodus 33:15) — God relented.

God did go with them. With the pillar of cloud and fire He remained their constant guide, moving when they moved and resting when they rested. His presence stayed in their very midst — dwelling in the Tabernacle, providing them with manna from heaven, and protecting them from hostile nations all the way to the Jordan River.

1. God’s Nearness in the Wilderness

During the wilderness wanderings, God’s nearness was constant, highly structured, and visible to all of Israel:

  • Speaking from the sanctuary. Leviticus begins with God immediately communicating His near presence, calling to Moses and speaking to him directly “from the tent of meeting” (Leviticus 1:1). Inside the Tabernacle, God met and spoke to Moses “from above the mercy seat… from between the two cherubim” (Numbers 7:89) on the Ark of the Testimony.
  • A camp built around His presence. The layout of the entire camp of Israel was physically centered on God. The twelve tribes pitched their tents facing the Tent of Meeting on every side, while the Levites camped directly around the Tabernacle to guard its sacred boundaries.
  • Maintaining holiness in the camp. Because God resided in their midst, the camp had to be kept strictly clean. God commanded the exclusion of defiling things, “that they may not defile their camp, in the midst of which I dwell” (Numbers 5:3). This extended even to basic physical sanitation — “the LORD your God walks in the midst of your camp, to save you… therefore your camp must be holy” (Deuteronomy 23:14).
  • Consuming fire and consecration. At the consecration of Aaron and the priesthood, God’s near presence was marked by a miraculous sign: “fire came forth from before the LORD and consumed the burnt offering… and when all the people saw it, they shouted and fell on their faces” (Leviticus 9:24). When Aaron’s sons Nadab and Abihu offered unholy fire, fire devoured them as a severe reminder of the reverence His nearness demanded: “I will show myself holy among those who are near me” (Leviticus 10:3).
  • The Day of Atonement. Because God dwelt in the midst of an unclean people, He established the Day of Atonement. Once a year the high priest would enter within the veil, sprinkling blood upon the mercy seat under a cloud of incense to cleanse the sanctuary — maintaining the Tent of Meeting, which “abides with them in the midst of their uncleannesses” (Leviticus 16:16).
  • The pillar of cloud and fire. Visibly, God guided the camp day and night. “On the day that the tabernacle was set up, the cloud covered the tabernacle… and in the evening it was over the tabernacle like the appearance of fire until morning” (Numbers 9:15). When the cloud arose, Israel set out; when it settled, they camped.
  • The Ark leading the way. Whenever the Ark of the Covenant set out to seek a resting place, Moses prayed, “Arise, O LORD, and let thy enemies be scattered” (Numbers 10:35) — and when it rested, “Return, O LORD, to the ten thousand thousands of Israel” (Numbers 10:36).
  • A God uniquely near. Reflecting on this wilderness journey, Moses marveled at the sheer intimacy of their relationship with God: “For what great nation is there that has a god so near to it as the LORD our God is to us, whenever we call upon him?” (Deuteronomy 4:7)

2. God’s Nearness in the Promised Land

Once Israel crossed into the Promised Land, the manifestation of God’s presence transitioned from a mobile wilderness sanctuary to a permanent geographic center:

  • Parting the Jordan. As they entered Canaan, the priests carried the Ark of the Covenant directly into the Jordan River. The waters parted, allowing the people to cross on dry ground.
  • Gilgal and Mount Ebal. They first encamped at Gilgal, where the wilderness manna ceased as they began eating the produce of the land. Shortly after, Joshua built an altar of unhewn stones on Mount Ebal, offering sacrifices and writing a copy of the Law in the presence of the assembly.
  • The sanctuary at Shiloh. Once the land was subdued, the congregation set up the Tent of Meeting at Shiloh, which served as Israel’s spiritual heart for generations. God later referred to Shiloh as “my place… where I made my name dwell at first” (Jeremiah 7:12).
  • Zion and the City of David. After David captured the stronghold of Zion, he grew increasingly powerful because “the LORD, the God of hosts, was with him” (2 Samuel 5:10). David subsequently brought the Ark of God up to Jerusalem with dancing, music, and sacrifices, pitching a tent for it in the City of David.
  • Solomon’s Temple. Solomon built a permanent Temple on Mount Moriah. At its dedication, “a cloud filled the house of the LORD, so that the priests could not stand to minister… for the glory of the LORD filled the house” (1 Kings 8:10–11).
  • The heavenly affirmation. When Solomon finished his dedicatory prayer, “fire came down from heaven and consumed the burnt offering… and the glory of the LORD filled the temple” (2 Chronicles 7:1). God consecrated the space, telling Solomon: “my eyes and my heart will be there for all time” (1 Kings 9:3).

3. Was Building the Temple the Final Step?

No — building the physical Temple was not the final step in God’s plan to dwell among His people. While magnificent, the Temple of stone and timber was a temporary, conditional shadow of a much deeper, internal nearness God intended to establish:

  • The Temple was conditional and temporary. God warned Solomon that if Israel turned away to serve other gods, He would pluck them out of the land and “this house which I have consecrated for my name I will cast out of my sight” (1 Kings 9:7), leaving it a heap of ruins. Because of centuries of unfaithfulness and abominations in the sanctuary, Ezekiel saw the glory of the LORD depart from the Temple (Ezekiel 10–11). The Temple was eventually burned to the ground by the Babylonians.
  • The Law written on the heart — the New Covenant. God’s ultimate goal was to move His dwelling place from a building of stone directly into human hearts. Through Jeremiah, God promised a New Covenant in which He would “put my law within them, and I will write it upon their hearts; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people” (Jeremiah 31:33). He promised to give them “one heart and one way” (Jeremiah 32:39) and “a heart to know that I am the LORD” (Jeremiah 24:7).
  • No more need for the Ark. Jeremiah prophesied a time when Israel would no longer seek, miss, or rebuild the Ark of the Covenant. Instead, “Jerusalem shall be called the throne of the LORD” (Jeremiah 3:16–17), and all nations would gather directly to His presence.
  • The Messianic Branch. The prophets pointed to a coming ruler called “the Branch” (Zechariah 6:12–13) who would build the ultimate, spiritual Temple of the Lord.
  • A wall of fire and glory within. Through Zechariah, God declared He would bypass physical structures entirely: “For I will be to her a wall of fire round about… and I will be the glory within her” (Zechariah 2:5) — and “for lo, I come and I will dwell in the midst of you, says the LORD” (Zechariah 2:10).
  • Ezekiel’s living Temple. Ezekiel’s final vision of a restored Temple depicts life-giving, healing waters flowing from its threshold (Ezekiel 47), concluding with the ultimate promise of God’s permanent presence in the city’s new name: “The LORD is there” (Ezekiel 48:35).

Ultimately, the physical Temple was a temporary anchor. God’s final plan of nearness was to establish an “everlasting covenant of peace” (Ezekiel 37:26) and set His sanctuary in the midst of His people forever — transforming His people themselves into His permanent dwelling place.

The wilderness journey wasn’t just about reaching a physical destination. It was a grueling, intimate masterclass in what it meant to walk in daily dependence on a holy God.

And that completes the journey I set out to trace in Part 1: a lovesick Father, drawing nearer step by step — the Garden, the Tabernacle, the Temple, and at last the promise of hearts He would write on and a city named The LORD Is There. The rest of the table — Immanuel, the torn veil, the Spirit within, and the day no temple is needed at all — is where all of this was heading. But that is the New Testament’s half of the story, and you already know how it ends: He came, He dwelt among us, and He is not finished drawing near.
