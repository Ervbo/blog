Leanness

A long time ago I noticed a pattern running through Scripture: some of God's harshest acts of judgment aren't punishments in the way we usually think of them — they're gifts. He gives His people exactly what they ask for, and the request itself becomes the judgment.

That idea is part of what helped me understand where pastor and author Josh Butler is coming from in his book The Skeletons in God's Closet, where he argues that hell is, in a sense, God simply giving people what they've wanted all along instead of Him.

I spent a while trying to recall a verse that captures this — something like "be careful what you wish (pray) for." I finally found it, and fittingly the Holy Spirit had me remembering it in the King James Version, which I don't read often:

"And he gave them their request; but sent leanness into their soul." — Psalm 106:15 (NKJV)

The Simpsons, of all things, understood this idea long before I put words to it. (Worth a watch.)

I did some follow-up the next morning — with a little help from AI, I'll admit — and pulled together a comparative table of the clearest examples of this pattern in Scripture.

PassageWhat was asked forHeart behind itWhat God grantedWhat followed
1 Samuel 8A human king "like the nations"Rejection of God's own kingship"Listen to their voice, make them a king" (8:22)Oppressive monarchy, exile — "In my anger I gave you a king" (Hosea 13:11)
Numbers 11 / Psalm 106:15Meat, and nostalgia for EgyptIngratitude, testing GodQuail "until it becomes loathsome" (Num 11:19–20)Plague while the meat was still in their teeth — "Kibroth-hattaavah," the graves of craving
Psalm 81:8–12Their own way, refusing to listenStubbornness of heart"I gave them over to the stubbornness of their heart" (81:12)Loss of the covenant blessing God offered instead
Romans 1:24–28Idolatry, the heart's own desiresWillful suppression of the truth about GodA threefold "God gave them over" — to lusts, dishonorable passions, a debased mindEscalating moral and relational disintegration

What strikes me across every one of these is that the gift itself is the rod — there's no separate punishment layered on top. The real target is always the heart, not just the behavior. And in Romans 1 and Psalm 81 especially, the worst judgment isn't something added — it's God simply withdrawing His restraint and stepping back. A good cross-reference for that same logic is 2 Thessalonians 2:10–12, where Paul applies it to those who "refused to love the truth."

I don't have a tidy conclusion for this one. It's just a pattern I keep running into, and it changes how I pray — I'm a little more careful now about what I ask God for, and a little more grateful when He says no.
