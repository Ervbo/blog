Confessing Sin. Of Others.

Something from our Bible study kept nagging at me this week, and I've thought about it on and off for a long time: how do I actually get better at identifying, confessing, and mourning the sins of my people — even when I'm not personally doing the specific things I'm mourning? Not the church down the street's sins, or the world's sins in some vague, self-righteous way — but the sins of the people I'm actually part of. My church. My community. My country. I want to be like Daniel and Ezekiel and David and Moses, who could stand in the gap and say "we," not "they."

I leaned on some help to get my thinking started, and while I don't think what came back is the final word on the subject, it gave me a real starting point. Here's what stuck with me most.

Scripture's godly leaders do three things at once when they carry the sin of their people. First, they see sin primarily as an offense against God's own glory — David weeps "because they do not keep your law" (Psalm 119:136), not because he's personally embarrassed by it. Second, they identify themselves with the people rather than standing over them. Nehemiah doesn't pretend he personally built every crumbling wall or committed every covenant-breaking act; he simply prays, "We have sinned against you… I and my father's house have sinned" (Nehemiah 1:6). Daniel, who by every account was personally righteous, still prays "we have sinned and done wrong… to us belongs open shame" (Daniel 9:5–8). Ezra tears his own clothes over sins he didn't commit (Ezra 9–10). And third — this is the part I need most — their grief never just sits there. It moves into intercession and action. Nehemiah's tears become a rebuilding plan. Paul's anguish for Israel (Romans 9:1–3) becomes evangelistic urgency.

That last point is where I tend to get stuck. It's easy for me to feel bad about the state of things — my church, my city, my country — and just stop there, in a kind of low-grade despair that doesn't actually cost me anything or lead anywhere. That's not what Daniel or Nehemiah modeled. Their grief always moved somewhere.

A practical shape I'm going to try: start by fixing my eyes on who God is (His holiness and His mercy, Daniel 9:4, Psalm 103:8–13), then confess my own sin honestly before I dare confess anyone else's, then move into "we" language for one circle at a time — family, church, city — naming one or two specific patterns rather than vague generalities. Then ask God plainly to share His grief with me rather than trying to manufacture emotion I don't have. And then, critically, turn that mourning into one concrete next step — a conversation, a change, an act of love — instead of letting it dissolve into nothing.

The guardrail worth naming: this isn't false guilt, and it isn't outrage dressed up as piety. There's a real difference between being offended because my preferences got stepped on and being grieved because God is offended and people are being destroyed by sin. I want the second one, not the first.

I don't have this figured out. But Psalm 106:6 gave me a place to start: "Both we and our fathers have sinned; we have committed iniquity; we have done wickedness." That's the posture I'm aiming for — standing inside the "we," not outside it looking down.
