Septuagint vs Tanakh

It's me again. This morning I had a pretty good Quiet Time — I think I guilted myself back on track after my last email confessing my lack of effort lately. I'm in Joshua now, in the middle chapters. There are three or four chapters where Joshua and Israel go around conquering the ethnic groups entrenched in wickedness in the land, and a phrase that kept recurring was that they killed “everyone” or left “no survivors.” That really stood out to me, and I'm certain God meant that phrase to drive home a point. You all know I'm a big believer in interpreting the Old Testament in light of the New (Rom 15:4, 1 Cor 10:1). God doesn't want me literally killing everyone in Folsom entrenched in wickedness — so what's the spiritual translation of these battles into today's Christian life? Whatever the battles are, they need a strategy, they need God's divine help for victory, and they're harsh and ruthless — no survivors allowed, no compromise, sustained until it's finished. Every square inch of the Promised Land was meant to be conquered and occupied for the Lord, even though in the actual history they only conquered about half of it. I know the answer to all this, I'm just too tired tonight to write it out.

But what hit me after my Quiet Time was a video clip about the Septuagint (LXX). Do you guys know much about it? I know a fair amount — I use it a lot in my Bible study, mostly through Blue Letter Bible or Bible Study Tools, and I've found it extremely useful. For example, we know Isaiah 7:14 prophesies the Messiah being born of a virgin. Some scholars push back, saying the Hebrew word is more naturally translated “young woman,” with no explicit virginity implied. Fair enough, I suppose — though knowing the Jewish culture God instituted in the Torah (Exodus, Leviticus, Deuteronomy especially), a “young woman” there was essentially equivalent to a virgin anyway. But just for fun I looked up what Greek word the Septuagint translators actually chose, and sure enough, they picked a stronger word than “young woman” and explicitly landed on “virgin.” I've had a lot of success finding things like that in the LXX.

However, now I'm nervous. I don't know if I should keep leaning on it the way I have. The video I watched raised a real doubt in my mind — does the same inspiration that applies to the Hebrew Old Testament and Greek New Testament apply to the Septuagint too? I'd stake my life on the inspiration of the Hebrew OT and Greek NT. But I've been sort of assuming the same rules apply to the LXX without really checking. So I need to caution you all that some of my earlier emails quoting the LXX — like when I pointed out kurios being used for the divine name — might lack the authority I assumed they had.

So I did some digging into how the two traditions actually differ. The short version: the Septuagint is a Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures made a couple centuries before Christ, widely used across the Hellenistic Jewish world and by the early church, while the Masoretic Text is the standardized Hebrew text finalized centuries later by Jewish scholars and remains authoritative for Rabbinic Judaism today. The two don't always agree — the LXX includes some additional books and passages not in the Hebrew canon, and here and there the wording itself differs, sometimes meaningfully, as with Isaiah 7:14. Both traditions carry real historical weight: the Masoretic Text preserves the Hebrew with remarkable care, while the Septuagint is the text most of the New Testament authors and early church fathers actually had in their hands and quoted from. That last point is what makes this question matter so much to me — if the LXX is what shaped so much of New Testament theology, its status can't be a throwaway detail.

I'm going to spend the next few days investigating this further. I need to know. If any of you already know the answer, tell me. — Erv
