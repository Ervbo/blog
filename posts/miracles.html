Biblical April 1, 2027

Miracles

Happy BSF day! Tonight I'm running an experiment: I'm only going to answer one of our discussion questions. I don't think I can actually pull it off, but I need to try — so much of my life has pivoted on “I just need to try harder,” and now when I tell myself that, I picture a screen door on a submarine.

Since I'm only answering one question (in theory), let me sneak in my own question first. It's about God and miracles — healing from cancer, a brain tumor, sciatica, any illness that's fatal or otherwise beyond restoring. Or, less dramatically, eating a 12x12 at In-N-Out and living to tell about it. Can the 49ers beat the Rams without their franchise quarterback and 90% of their roster on injured reserve — on their own, or does God need to arrange a missed field goal, a fumble at the one-yard line, a terrible kickoff in overtime?

Every Christian believes God can do miracles. I remember my manager at Intel half-mocking me once: “Do you really believe the Bible? You believe Jonah was swallowed by a great fish?” I said, absolutely. I believe God created the universe from nothing, parted the Red Sea, that Jesus was born of a virgin, lived sinless, was crucified, died, and rose again, that He calmed storms and healed every kind of sickness. Given all that, Jonah surviving inside a fish is a small ask — and honestly, I'll go further: I think it's just as likely he actually died in there and God raised him back to life before the fish spit him out, given how directly Jesus ties Jonah's three days and nights to His own three days in the heart of the earth. If God can speak the universe into being, that settles it for me on every miracle that comes after.

The real disagreement among Christians isn't whether God can do miracles — it's whether He still does them. Does He still heal the blind? The leper? This comes up a lot around spiritual gifts — healing, miracles, prophecy, tongues, interpretation of tongues, discerning spirits, faith. One large group within the church holds that these gifts ceased, pointing to 1 Corinthians 13:10 (“when the time of perfection comes, these partial things will become useless”) and reading “the time of perfection” as the completion of the biblical canon. Another large group holds that God still does all of these things without exception, pointing to verses like Mark 11:24, James 5:16–18, and Matthew 13:58, and sometimes concluding that if God doesn't act, it's because our faith was lacking.

Here's my actual question: both groups genuinely believe God is, by His very nature, a God of miracles — doing one is like breathing to Him. The difference is that one group believes there's no promise He will act on any given request, that it's entirely His discretion; the other believes He will do whatever is asked, and that this confidence is itself where the real faith lies. I think about this every time I read Daniel 3:16–18. Daniel's friends tell the king, in effect: our God is a God of miracles, He can save us from your furnace, from a den of hungry lions, even after you've soaked us in gasoline — but if He doesn't, that's fine too, He must have His reasons. Would it have shown more faith for them to instead say, “Our God is omnipotent, there's nothing He can't do, and He will save us no matter what”? That's the actual question.

Right before and after my thirteen-hour surgery, I had Christians stop by to pray for me, and it was an odd experience. I wasn't even sure what to pray for myself. The prayers I heard split almost exactly along these same two lines — one group focused on God's greatness and then added something like an escape clause, in case He didn't act. The other focused on His omnipotence and then celebrated that my healing had already happened, which made me wonder why I was still headed into surgery at all.

Scripture says it's impossible to please God without faith (Heb 11:6). Do both groups have faith? Was there a real difference in the quality of it? I don't think Daniel and his friends' “even if He does not save us” showed any less faith — if anything, I don't think believing a miracle has already happened is evidence of truer or greater faith than trusting God's will either way. Mark 11:24 seems to suggest otherwise on the surface, but it doesn't sit right with me against everything else in Scripture. It makes more sense to me read alongside Jesus's own words — not my will but Thine be done — and alongside Philippians 4:13. When someone claims “I can do all things through Christ” to mean 400 completions and 4,000 yards and a 100+ QB rating this season, I think the verse actually means: I can do all things that are God's will through Christ who strengthens me. Like prayer, God can do any miracle — when it's His will.

That's about it. — Erv
