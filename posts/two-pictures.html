Two Pictures and an Unlocked Door

A coda to the God Draws Near series.

Right after I posted the Lovesick sermon, I came across two true stories I wish I had found a week earlier. They would have been perfect for it. Both are about a runaway daughter and a parent who will not stop searching — and both have been retold by better storytellers than me, so I will give you the short version in my own words and point you to theirs.

The first is a story Max Lucado tells in No Wonder They Call Him the Savior, about a widowed mother named Maria and her daughter Christina, in a poor village in Brazil. Christina dreamed of the big city, and one morning her mother woke to an empty bed. Maria knew where her daughter had gone, and she knew — the way only a mother knows — what a penniless girl too proud to come home would end up doing to survive there. So before boarding the bus to Rio de Janeiro, she stopped at a drugstore photo booth and spent what little she had on pictures of herself. She spent her days in the city leaving those pictures everywhere a girl in trouble might pass — hotel lobbies, nightclub mirrors, phone booths — until the money and the photos ran out, and she went home. Weeks later, Christina, worn down and far from the girl who had left, came down a hotel staircase and saw a familiar face taped to the lobby mirror. On the back of the photo, in her mother’s hand: “Whatever you have done, whatever you have become, it doesn’t matter. Please come home.” She did.

The second is a story Robert Strand tells in his Moments to Give series, from Glasgow, Scotland. A teenage daughter rejected her parents’ faith and their home — “I don’t want your God” — and disappeared into the streets, where the years took her the same hard direction. Her father died. Her mother grew old. And then the mother did something very like Maria: she went down to skid row and asked every rescue mission to hang a picture of her — a smiling, gray-haired woman — with one handwritten line beneath it: “I love you still … come home!” One day the daughter wandered into a mission for a meal, saw the picture, and walked through the night to get home. When she arrived at dawn, the front door swung open at her touch, and she ran in terrified that someone had broken in. Her mother’s answer is the whole gospel in one sentence: “From the day you left, that door has never been locked.” (Strand opens his telling with a short poem worth looking up on its own — “Mother’s Covers” by Dona Maddux Cooper, about a mother who once covered her small child with blankets and now covers her grown one with prayer.)

Do you see why I wish I had these for the sermon? These mothers are doing exactly what the lovesick Father does. The picture left where the runaway will pass is Genesis 3:15 — a promise planted in the wreckage, waiting to be found. The note on the back is the everlasting covenant: whatever you have done, it doesn’t matter to My love — come home. And the door that was never locked, not for one day in all those years — that is the torn veil. The daughter thought she would have to knock, explain, and earn her way back in. The way had been open the entire time. All that was ever left to do was walk through it.

“For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost.” — Luke 19:10
