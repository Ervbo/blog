Sola Scriptura?

TGIF — or really, TGIAD, Thank God It's Any Day. Are you familiar with the term Sola Scriptura? I am, but not deeply, and I think it's worth a look if you aren't — here's a good starting point. I don't want to wade into the nuances that come up between Catholics and Protestants on this, but I do want to share my own thinking on a related question: what do we actually need beyond the Bible to grow spiritually, in terms of written resources? For Catholics and Protestants alike, I think the honest answer is nothing. But I want to add two clarifications. First, that doesn't mean we shouldn't use other Bible resources. Second, it doesn't mean there's no other source of absolute truth out there. Let me explain both.

I want to draw a line between “resources” and “books.” By resources, I mean tools that amplify the meaning of what we already have in the Bible's original languages — things like a concordance, an interlinear, Hebrew or Greek language instruction, a Bible or secular dictionary, an encyclopedia, cross-references, topical summaries, an atlas, maps, study Bibles, and commentaries. Given the sheer number of surviving manuscripts and the work of textual criticism, I'm convinced that when it comes to actually knowing God and relating to Him, the Bible stands alone — Sola Scriptura. But these resources exist to help us take full advantage of what's already there, not to add to it. Study Bibles and commentaries live in a bit of a gray area, but used properly they shouldn't add anything beyond what the original author intended. Bible software falls squarely into the “resource” category too.

By “books,” I mean everything that's more about the Bible than a tool for studying it directly — devotionals, missionary biographies, fiction (Wisdom Hunter), fiction based on Scripture (the Left Behind series), Christian living authors like Yancey and Lucado, and what I'll call “expressionals” — poetry, hymnals, worship material. That leaves a few categories I'm still sorting — children's Bibles, paraphrased Bibles, theology, doctrine, and apologetics. My instinct is that theology, doctrine, and apologetics belong more with “resources,” and the other two with “books,” but I'm still thinking it through.

Here's the part that might be more controversial — or at least I've never heard anyone put it quite this way. I've been a math kid since childhood, then an engineer, then a rocket scientist, and I've always been drawn to truth, to absolutes, to absolute truth. I've never once seen a genuine conflict between the Bible and science — not once. I started reading the Bible cover to cover at 17, and started my engineering education at 18, and never at any point did I think Genesis was wrong because of some scientific finding, or that 2+2 no longer equaled 4, or that F no longer equaled m times a. Later in life I picked up a phrase that I've embraced completely: all truth is God's truth, and there is no other kind.

I recently watched a 21-minute video from a Bible teacher that covers a lot of what I want to say here better than I could myself — worth a watch if you have the time: watch here. His basic message: if you want to get more out of your Bible study, there are genuinely incredible tools available to you now. A quick sketch of the path he lays out — start with Strong's Exhaustive Concordance to find where a word occurs and its underlying Hebrew or Greek. Be careful here, though: pulling a definition out of context is a real trap. “Thou shall not muzzle the ox while he is threshing” (1 Cor 9:9) has been twisted out of context to argue all sorts of things that have nothing to do with what Paul actually meant. That's where an interlinear comes in — go to the passage, find the English word, then the corresponding Greek word, then take that word to a proper lexicon like BDAG, either in hard copy or through Bible software.
