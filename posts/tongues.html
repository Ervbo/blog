Tongues. When. Why.

If I were to ask you, “What is the one issue that divides the body of Christ more than any other?” — would you not say it is speaking in tongues? Looking back on my Christian life, that would certainly be the answer for me. I became a Christian at 17 years old. I did not grow up in a Christian home. Far from it. I knew absolutely nothing about Christianity when I heard the gospel for the first time. I had not even heard the term ‘Christian,’ really. I thought it had to do with a group of people fighting in Northern Ireland. A friend of a friend led me to Christ, and I will be forever thankful for him. But there was no real follow-up. I was left to myself. All I had was a paraphrase of the NT given to me by the girl I took to the Junior Prom. I am glad I held onto it, even though I can’t remember why I did. Fortunately, I instinctively knew I should begin reading it. Which I did. Right away. And a lot.

I didn’t go to church for quite a while, because I did not know why it was important or even where I would go if I went. It was just me and my paraphrase of the NT. In many ways I would not have had it any other way. I was insulated from being swayed by the teachings of the different denominations. I did not need to hear a sermon and have that be my spiritual nourishment. So this issue of speaking in tongues did not even cross my path until I got into college. I believe it was in my first year there that I began listening to sermons by Chuck Swindoll and John MacArthur. I would buy almost all their radio messages on cassette. I had hundreds of them then, and still do. I recall an extended teaching series that MacArthur did on the Charismatics. I remember that for a couple of reasons. A) Because when I bought the cassettes, it was a 12-volume set, which was atypical for him. B) Because of the intensity of his messages. It was obvious to even a neophyte like me that this series was more than sermons on Christian living — this was hitting on a nerve.

Also at that time, a couple of good Christian friends of mine started going to a church called Davis Evangelical. I did not know much about it. As far as I knew, most Christians in the university went to either the FBC or the Covenant church. All I knew about the church was that it was charismatic. Which, even that, did not register too deeply with me. I recall talking to them about the church — how they liked it, how it was different, and why they chose to go there. Quickly they zeroed in on the Baptism of the Spirit and speaking in tongues. I asked what the baptism of the Spirit was, and what speaking in tongues had to do with anything. They were eager to answer that, and basically said: “everything!” They told me that when I became a Christian, I received the Holy Spirit but not the fullness of the Spirit. Not quite sure how they put it, but it was to the effect that the Spirit was with me but not in me. From what I could tell, it was as if the Holy Spirit was in my life — but only in my guest house. I had never heard anything like that and was genuinely confused. I asked them how to get this ‘extra’ baptism of the Spirit. I think they said that I needed to go to their church and have everyone lay hands on me until it happened. I did not understand why anyone would need to do that, but I asked, “How will I know when it happens?” That is when they told me: I would know when I spoke in tongues. Now I understood why they emphasized the two together. In charismatic theology, the two are inseparable.

Now I was fully immersed in this divisive issue. Not only was I listening to this long 12-hour series on the charismatics and what they believe — I had two good friends who were doing their best to persuade me that I needed to do the same to be ‘fully’ a Christian. Additionally, MacArthur turned his teaching series on the subject into a book, and I was also reading that. So I was wrestling with this in a big way. Little did I know then that I would be wrestling with it almost my entire Christian life! I encountered similar friends and similar teachings everywhere I went.

The questions on the table

Let me get to the main issue(s) now, and why I decided to write this post. Let me define what the problem is and what questions appear to be unanswered. 1) Is there a baptism of the Spirit? Better yet, is there a second baptism of the Spirit needed to be saved, or even just to be more empowered as a believer? 2) If so, how is it obtained? What does a believer need to do to receive it? 3) Similarly, what is speaking in tongues? How is it obtained? What does a believer need to do to have that gift? For this post I am only going to address this last question. (The first two deserve — and will get — their own post.)

Tongues in Acts

What is speaking in tongues? To answer this, there are only two sections of the Bible that need to be looked at. The main passages are Acts 2, 10, and 19, and 1 Corinthians 12–14. When we look at the three chapters of Acts, we see that speaking in tongues was a gift given to the early church to share the gospel of Christ with those from other cultures who spoke a different language. There might be some question as to whether the tongues in chapter 19 are known languages, but given the context of Acts — that was the only kind of tongue observed — I think it is a safe assumption. So far, so good.

Tongues in 1 Corinthians

Now let’s look at the three chapters in 1 Corinthians. The first places we see speaking in tongues are the three passages below. In context, Paul is talking about how the Holy Spirit gives special abilities to every believer. To explain it further, he uses the analogy of a human body: each part of our body has a special ability, and all parts of the body are needed for a body to be fully functional and healthy. He combines the two ideas together and tells them, “You are the body of Christ.” All of you. Working together. Interdependent.

“Still another person is given the ability to speak in unknown languages, while another is given the ability to interpret what is being said. It is the one and only Spirit who distributes all these gifts. He alone decides which gift each person should have.” (1 Corinthians 12:10–11)

“Here are some of the parts God has appointed for the church… those who speak in unknown languages.” (12:28)

“Do we all have the ability to speak in unknown languages? Do we all have the ability to interpret unknown languages? Of course not! So you should earnestly desire the most helpful gifts.” (12:30–31)

That is also simple enough to understand. But here we learn something different from what we saw in Acts. In Acts, it appears that every believer there spoke in tongues, and the ‘unknown’ language was simply unknown to the speaker. In 1 Corinthians we see that not every believer speaks in an unknown tongue, and that it is a special ability, like other parts of the body. In addition, we now see there is also another gifting that allows for the interpretation of these unknown tongues. This implies that this tongue itself is unknown — not of this world — and needs a special interpretation, not just a listener who happens to speak that language.

The following verses in chapters 13 and 14 go into further detail. Here I will just make some simple comments within [brackets]:

“If I could speak all the languages of earth and of angels [this fits exactly the distinction we are seeing — tongues for evangelism (of the earth) and tongues for edification (of angels)], but didn’t love others, I would only be a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal.” (13:1)

“Prophecy and speaking in unknown languages and special knowledge will become useless [this is important — it’s as if Paul knew this would become a divisive issue, and reminded them that love is the only issue worth fighting over]. But love will last forever!” (13:8)

“For if you have the ability to speak in tongues, you will be talking only to God, since people won’t be able to understand you [again, this is different from the tongue spoken to people for evangelism]. You will be speaking by the power of the Spirit, but it will all be mysterious [the tongues in Acts were not mysterious]. But one who prophesies strengthens others, encourages them, and comforts them. A person who speaks in tongues is strengthened personally, but one who speaks a word of prophecy strengthens the entire church [the tongues in Acts did not strengthen anyone personally — they translated the gospel for the hearers].” (14:2–4)

“I wish you could all speak in tongues, but even more I wish you could all prophesy. For prophecy is greater than speaking in tongues, unless someone interprets what you are saying so that the whole church will be strengthened [like the tongues in Acts, the value comes from the interpretation: in Acts the evangelistic value was that the listener’s own spoken language was the interpretation; in 1 Corinthians the edifying value is that another believer with that gift is the interpretation].” (14:5)

“Dear brothers and sisters, if I should come to you speaking in an unknown language, how would that help you? But if I bring you a revelation or some special knowledge or prophecy or teaching, that will be helpful.” (14:6)

“It’s the same for you. If you speak to people in words they don’t understand, how will they know what you are saying? You might as well be talking into empty space [again, see the similarity: in Acts the hearers understood what the disciples were saying; in 1 Corinthians the church will understand if there is an interpreter].” (14:9)

“There are many different languages in the world, and every language has meaning. But if I don’t understand a language, I will be a foreigner to someone who speaks it, and the one who speaks it will be a foreigner to me. And the same is true for you. Since you are so eager to have the special abilities the Spirit gives, seek those that will strengthen the whole church [like the comment on love — Paul seems to be anticipating conflict of another kind, and reminds them the focus is always on the Lord, and others. Not ourselves].” (14:10–12)

When. Why. And how much it should divide us

So here is my answer to the question this post set out to ask. When? In Acts — at the border crossings of the gospel, when the message leapt from one language to the next. Why? Two different purposes for two different settings: a known language for evangelism (of the earth), and an unknown one for edification (of angels) — and the second is one gift among many, given to some and not others, by the Spirit who “alone decides.” And one more thing the chart below says better than I can: this question — whether the gifts continue, and what kind of tongues these are — sits in Tiers 2 and 3 of Christian doctrine. It shapes which church we worship in. It is worth studying hard, which is why I wrote this. But it is not Tier 1. The friends who laid hands at Davis Evangelical and the neophyte with his MacArthur cassettes were — and are — brothers.

The baptism-of-the-Spirit questions — is there a second one, and how is it received — still stand open from my list above. That post is coming. Until then: whichever tier you worship in, 1 Corinthians 13 sits between the two tongues chapters on purpose. Paul put the love chapter in the middle of the fight. — Erv
