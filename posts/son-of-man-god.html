The Dust and the Throne

This is the written analysis behind the slide deck I shared in Son of Man. Son of God. — the study that produced it, pulled together from my materials in NotebookLM and organized here.

“I saw in the night visions, and, behold, one like the Son of man came with the clouds of heaven, and came to the Ancient of days… And there was given him dominion, and glory, and a kingdom, that all people, nations, and languages, should serve him.” — Daniel 7:13–14

The title “Son of Man” is the primary designation Jesus used for himself, appearing over 80 times across the four Gospels. While disciples, crowds, and even demons reached for the traditional titles — Messiah, Son of David, Son of God — Jesus consistently reached for this specific phrase. It is a linguistic and theological paradox that fuses the lowest form of human mortality with the highest claim to divine authority. By using a term that functioned as a humble idiom in everyday Aramaic while simultaneously invoking the most exalted visions in Hebrew Scripture, Jesus communicated his identity with strategic nuance — hiding his highest claims in plain sight until his final trial.

The low thread: Ezekiel and human mortality

In the Hebrew idiom ben adam, “Son of Man” refers simply to a human being — a mortal made of dust. In the Book of Ezekiel, God addresses the prophet as “Son of Man” over 90 times, and the timing is the point: the title comes immediately after Ezekiel witnesses the overwhelming glory of God. It stands as a reminder of his status — he is dust that God chose to speak to, not a divine or glorious being. In that context, “Son of Man” is the smallest possible way to describe a human being in the presence of the infinite.

The high thread: Daniel and divine authority

A few books later, Daniel presents a radically different vision of the same phrase. Daniel 7 depicts a figure “like a son of man” approaching the Ancient of Days on the clouds of heaven — a mode of transport reserved for God alone throughout the Old Testament. This figure is granted dominion, glory, and a kingdom that is everlasting and will never be destroyed. And the Aramaic text specifies that all nations should serve him — using a word for the reverence owed to deity alone, not the ordinary service given to a human king.

Hiding in plain sight

Jesus’ preference for “Son of Man” over titles like “Messiah” or “Son of God” was a masterclass in strategic communication. In the first century, “Messiah” was loaded — it implied a nationalist deliverer, a military general who would overthrow Roman rule, and using it publicly would have incited an uprising prematurely. “Son of God” was a direct claim to divinity that the religious leaders monitored closely for blasphemy.

“Son of Man” carried neither liability on its surface. In everyday Aramaic (bar enash), the phrase could simply mean “someone” or “a man like me” — an ordinary, flexible idiom that could pass right over a crowd’s head as a humble self-reference. But to those who knew the Scriptures — specifically Daniel 7 — the title was an electrifying claim to be the one who would reign alongside God.

Playing both notes

Throughout his ministry, Jesus played both notes of the title simultaneously. He claimed divine prerogatives with it: the “Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins” — a power the religious elite believed belonged to God alone — and the “Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath,” claiming authority over an institution established at creation. And he embraced human limitation with the very same words: the “Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head,” and the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected, and die. While “Messiah” was expected to win, the Son of Man — as a man — could suffer and die without breaking the title’s inherent meaning.

The Sanhedrin trial: the final reveal

The two threads — the prisoner in the dust and the King on the throne — collided at Jesus’ trial before the Sanhedrin. The high priest asked directly: “Are you the Messiah, the Son of the Blessed?” Jesus replied, “I am,” and immediately invoked Daniel 7: “You will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of power and coming with the clouds of heaven.”

Standing in chains — the lowest person in the room — Jesus claimed the highest possible status. The high priest tore his robes, recognizing the statement as the severest blasphemy: a bound prisoner was claiming the throne next to God. And one more detail worth pondering: the Greek participle for “coming” suggests a continuous, unfolding motion. The suffering and the glory — the cross and the throne — were not two separate stories, but one singular motion.

Why it matters

The title “Son of Man” allowed Jesus to be both near enough to have nowhere to lay his head and strong enough to hold every nation before his throne. By choosing the only title that could encompass both the dust of human experience (Ezekiel) and the throne of divine dominion (Daniel), Jesus identified fully with the human condition while maintaining his claim to eternal authority. He did not ask his followers to choose between a God who is close and a God who is powerful. The title “Son of Man” insists that he is both.
