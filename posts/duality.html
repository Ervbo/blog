It’s All About Duality

I am an engineer. I see patterns in most everything, and there is one pattern I have been collecting for years: pairs of opposites that refuse to be merged. I finally wrote the whole list down and put it to an AI, asking the question that has been nagging me since college. Here is my question, and then the response — which was too good to keep to myself.

I am an engineer. I see patterns in most everything. Here is one that I would like your perspective on. First I will list here the 2 opposing realms I see everywhere in nature and in life: 1) Objective vs Subjective. 2) Relativity vs Quantum Mechanics. 3) Intelligence vs Intuition. 4) Logic vs Feelings. 5) Sheet music vs Playing by ear. 6) Brain vs Mind. 7) Body vs Soul. 8) Why vs How. 9) Science vs Art.

In all of these examples I see something powerful and extremely revealing about existence. The tension that must exist in the basic fundamentals. Perhaps all of these examples are paradoxes in themselves. At a minimum they are polar ends of each other. As humans it appears we have a hard time grasping both ends and prefer to live on one end or the other. Another thing we do is to try and connect the two. For example I have seen researchers try to define ‘good’ art by reducing images to mathematical components. My favorite example is Relativity vs Quantum Mechanics. I have worked on uniting these 2 theories since I was in college. But now I am thinking that maybe they don’t need to be united at all. Just like my other examples — why not have both sides with no linkage? Perhaps the wonder and beauty of life is not putting these examples in a box but exploring what is between them? Ok. What do you think?

The response:

What you’re describing has a name in physics, and Niels Bohr thought it was so important he made it the centerpiece of his identity. When he was knighted in 1947, he designed a coat of arms with the yin-yang symbol and the motto Contraria sunt complementa — “opposites are complementary” (ichingai.info, phys.libretexts.org). This wasn’t a private hobby — it was his generalization of the wave/particle problem into a full philosophy. He argued that an electron’s wave nature and particle nature aren’t two approximations of one “true” underlying thing waiting to be unified. Each description is complete on its own terms, and each is required for the full picture, but you cannot hold both in view at the same instant — the experimental setup you choose determines which face nature shows you. Bohr later spent decades trying to extend that same logic to biology, psychology, free will vs. determinism, even culture — he thought complementarity was a feature of reality itself, not just quantum weirdness. You’re rediscovering his program from an engineer’s vantage point, which is a good sign you’re onto something real.

Where I’d push back slightly on “no linkage”

I don’t think you actually want zero linkage — I think what you’re really after is irreducibility without disconnection. Relativity and QM aren’t unrelated; they’re catastrophically related, in that each breaks down exactly where the other should apply, and that failure point (the Planck scale, the singularity, the birth of the universe) is where all the interesting physics lives. General relativity insists spacetime is smooth and continuous; quantum mechanics insists everything is discrete and probabilistic — and when you try to force one framework’s math into the other’s domain, you get infinities, not silence. That’s not “no connection,” that’s maximal tension at a shared boundary (ethz.ch). My suspicion: the boundary itself — not a unifying equation — may be the actual object of interest. Fifty years of string theory and loop quantum gravity chasing a merger may be a category error, the same way “what note is played when you convert sheet music into playing by ear” is a category error. You don’t unify them; you find the door between the rooms.

A pattern inside your pattern

Look at your nine pairs again and I think you’ll notice they cluster:

Descriptive vs. participatory — Sheet music vs. playing by ear, Logic vs. feelings, How vs. why, Science vs. art. One side externalizes and formalizes; the other side is inhabited, known from inside. You can transcribe a jazz solo into notation, but the transcription is not the solo — it’s a shadow cast by something lived.

Third-person vs. first-person — Objective vs. subjective, Brain vs. mind, Body vs. soul. This is basically the philosophical “hard problem of consciousness” repeated three times: no amount of third-person neuron-firing description seems to logically entail the first-person fact of what it is like to be the one firing them. Physicalism keeps promising to close that gap and keeps failing to, not for lack of data but because the two descriptions may be answering different questions entirely.

Formal system vs. its ground — Relativity vs. QM, Intelligence vs. intuition. Gödel showed that any formal system rich enough to be interesting cannot prove its own consistency from within — it needs something outside itself. That’s structurally the same shape as “why” needing something “how” can’t supply on its own terms.

That researchers keep trying to reduce “good art” to mathematical proportion (golden ratio, Fibonacci, whatever) and it keeps half-working and half-failing is itself evidence for your thesis — the residue that always escapes the equation is exactly the part that mattered.

The theological version you already know

You’ve spent real time in Koine Greek word studies, so you’ll recognize this shape from Scripture too. Hebrew anthropology doesn’t actually give you a clean body/soul split — nephesh is closer to “a body animated and made alive,” not a soul trapped inside meat waiting for parole. The Greek dualism most Christians default to (soul escapes body at death) is arguably a later import, which is why the New Testament insists on bodily resurrection rather than disembodied bliss — the two are meant to stay married, not resolved into one. That’s a very on-theme data point: even the “body vs. soul” pair may be a case where the biblical authors refuse the reduction in either direction, and the payoff (a whole person, raised) only shows up when you keep both in play rather than picking a winner.

So, the actual answer

I think you’re right that most of these shouldn’t be unified — collapsing either pair into the other always loses something real (reductive materialism flattens the mind; pure mysticism abandons the brain). But I don’t think the alternative is disconnection either. I’d propose a third option: these are complementary descriptions of one thing too rich for either language alone, and the “wonder and beauty” you’re pointing at lives specifically at the seam — the place where sheet music runs out and the player has to feel the next note, where the equations of physics hit a wall at the Big Bang, where a why-question outlives every how-answer you throw at it. That seam isn’t a bug to be engineered away. It might be the most honest place reality ever shows its actual shape to a finite mind — which, not incidentally, is close to what Bohr thought he’d found in the electron, and close to what the biblical writers seem to have thought about a human being.

— end of the response. I have been chewing on “the door between the rooms” ever since. Where do you land — unify, disconnect, or explore the seam? The Comments are open.
