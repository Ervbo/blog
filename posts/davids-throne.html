David's throne

BSF Lesson 2, Question 12 asks how the prophecy in Ezekiel 37:24–26 points to Christ — and pointed us back to 2 Samuel 7:12–16, Jeremiah 33:17, and Matthew 1:1. Our group spent a good chunk of the discussion arguing over something more basic underneath the question itself: does David personally reign forever on his own throne, or is it someone else — meaning Christ — who ultimately occupies it?

I lean on what I keep calling “Correlation” — reading Scripture with Scripture — and by that method David is best understood as a type of Christ, not a literal, eternal occupant of his own throne.

The Old Testament promise itself is remarkably specific. God tells David, “Your house and your kingdom shall be established forever before you; your throne shall be established forever” (2 Samuel 7:16). Psalm 89 restates it twice — his seed shall endure forever, his throne as the sun before God (Ps 89:35–37) — and Jeremiah 33:17 promises David “shall never lack a man to sit on the throne of the house of Israel.” Ezekiel 37:25, the verse behind our question, makes the same promise about “my servant David” being their prince forever.

Taken alone, that's a strange promise — David died, and no descendant of his has physically sat on a throne in Jerusalem for roughly 2,600 years. The New Testament resolves the tension by identifying who “David” ultimately points to. Isaiah had already said the child to come would sit “upon the throne of David…forever” (Isa 9:7). Daniel's “son of man” is given “a kingdom that will never be destroyed” (Dan 7:13–14). Jeremiah's “righteous Branch” who reigns is a future descendant, not David reborn (Jer 23:5). And the angel tells Mary plainly that her son “will reign over the house of Jacob forever,” and “the Lord God will give him the throne of his father David” (Luke 1:32–33).

Matthew opens his gospel by calling Jesus “the son of David” for exactly this reason (Matt 1:1) — not a poetic nod, but a genealogical and theological claim: Jesus is the one in whom the promise to David is finally, permanently fulfilled. David was never meant to occupy that throne forever in his own body. He was always pointing forward to someone who would.

For anyone who wants to dig further into the historical and theological case, Biblehub's commentary on 2 Samuel 7:16, the Institute for Creation Research's “The Throne of David”, and GotQuestions' “What is the throne of David that Jesus is supposed to sit on?” all walk through the Davidic covenant and its fulfillment in Christ from slightly different angles.
