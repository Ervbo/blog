Born Again.

After my recent post asking Are You a Christian? my mind continued thinking on the subject. I mentioned that by the grace of God all that we must do to be forgiven and made ‘right’ in God’s eyes is to ‘believe in’ Him. That alone is sort of mind-bending. Unless God opens our eyes to understand it, our flesh will reject that idea. Perhaps it’s our pride or our sense of justice, but we insist that something must be ‘done’ instead. Something did have to be done — and again, by the grace of God, it was done by none other than God the Son, Jesus Himself. ‘Believing in’ Him and what He did links us to Him and what was accomplished on our behalf.

As I thought more on this, I realized I did not go into detail as to everything that changes when we ‘believe in’ Christ. I touched on the fact that it must be linked to more than saying just a prayer of ‘I believe in You.’ I also touched on the fact that it must be linked to more than things you have done that are considered Christian activities. But today I want to explore that linkage much deeper. I believe the best way to do that is to go back to John 3 and see what Jesus said beforehand to a religious ruler named Nicodemus. A Pharisee. Who had come to talk to Jesus at night, privately, so no one would see him. After the miracle of Jesus turning the water into wine in Cana and then clearing the temple of the merchants and money changers in Jerusalem, the Bible tells us that many ‘began to trust in Him’ (John 2:23). To ‘believe in’ Him.

Essentially Nicodemus came to Jesus to answer the very question we are considering here. What is this linkage? Is it just a mental conviction, a promise to keep, a new covenant with God? Before Nicodemus is even able to ask the question, Jesus answers it. Btw — this is a fun study to do. Look up all the verses where Jesus does not exactly answer the question asked of Him, or bypasses it altogether to get at what is behind the question. When you are God the Son you can do cool stuff like that. It saves everyone lots of time and effort.

Here is what He said in John 3:3:

“Jesus replied, ‘I tell you the truth, unless you are born again, you cannot see the Kingdom of God.’” (John 3:3, NLT)

The answer is clear. Direct and to the point. ‘Believing in’ Jesus immediately links us to a new birth. Very clear and very direct. But once again, not easy to understand at first. Here is what Nicodemus says in John 3:4:

“‘What do you mean?’ exclaimed Nicodemus. ‘How can an old man go back into his mother’s womb and be born again?’” (John 3:4, NLT)

At this point you might be thinking — Am I a Christian? Yes, I believe in Christ. What do you mean you ‘believe in’ Him? I believe that He is all that the Bible says about Him and what He says about Himself. Why He is truly the Messiah and why He came to lay down His life. Am I born again? I am not sure. I don’t even know what that question means.

Rather than me giving a summary of it, I think we should roll up our sleeves a little and put on our Bible Study hats. Just as we did with ‘believe in,’ let’s look at ‘born again.’ Go through the notes below (from my Word Study app) and understand everything that is being said in these verses. I will meet you afterwards on the other side of it, and we can discuss it further.

BORN — Greek: γεννάω (gennaō)

Pronunciation · Strong’s · Frequency
gen-NAH-oh · G1080 · appears 97 times in the New Testament

Key passage
“Jesus answered him, ‘Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God.’” — John 3:3

Biblical meaning
To be begotten or brought into a new existence — especially the spiritual birth from above that Jesus described to Nicodemus. This birth is not of human will but of God, granting new life and new identity.

Strong’s definition
To procreate (properly, of the father, but by extension of the mother); figuratively, to regenerate.

Word origin
From genos (kind, offspring). Gennaō means to father, beget, or give birth — entering a new family line or nature.

Original language insight
The Greek word gennaō carries a rich semantic range that includes not only the act of giving birth but also the broader concept of begetting or bringing forth life. It encompasses both physical and metaphorical birth, indicating origin, lineage, and even spiritual rebirth. Reading it in Greek reveals layers of meaning, such as the intimate relationship between the begetter and the begotten, which is often lost in the simple English translation ‘born.’ This depth enhances our understanding of passages like John 3:3, where Jesus speaks of being ‘born again.’

Sentence structure
In key biblical passages, gennaō often appears in the aorist tense, which conveys a completed action, emphasizing the definitive nature of the birth or begetting event. It is typically used in the active voice, highlighting the agency of the one who begets. This grammatical structure underscores the transformative and decisive moment of birth or spiritual rebirth, as seen in Jesus’ conversation with Nicodemus about being ‘born of water and the Spirit.’

Life in biblical times
In the ancient world, gennaō was deeply tied to concepts of family lineage and identity. Birth determined one’s place in society and inheritance rights. The word would evoke thoughts of continuity and belonging within a family or community. In a spiritual context, it also suggested a new identity and relationship with God, resonating with the cultural understanding of being part of a divine family through spiritual rebirth.

Did you know?
Jesus told Nicodemus a grown man must be ‘born again’ to see God’s kingdom (John 3:3). The Greek allows ‘born from above’ — birth sourced in heaven, not merely a moral fresh start.

Theological significance
Flesh gives birth to flesh; the Spirit gives birth to spirit (John 3:6). Regeneration is monergistic — God’s work — yet received through faith. Born of God, we become children of God with a new nature.

Apply it
When were you born again — and does your life show it? New birth produces new desires. Ask God to strengthen whatever feels still ‘unborn’ in your obedience, love, or courage.

Related words · Explore in Scripture
Birth (gennēsis), Spirit (pneuma), Child (teknon) · John 3:3; John 1:13; 1 Peter 1:23

Meeting you on the other side

There. What do you think of all that? What stands out to you? What did you learn, and what did you see that you didn’t before?

Here is what I see. As Nicodemus almost gets it, Jesus is saying to be born again — but not another physical birth. We have all done that. We became physical humans. We behave like humans. We have physical traits. But Jesus is talking about a spiritual birth that is also needed. Which is linked to ‘believing in’ Him that the people in Cana and Jerusalem were doing. And what Nicodemus himself was considering. He must be born again spiritually. He must become a spiritual person. Behave like a spiritual person. Have spiritual traits. To be saved you must have both.

Next, I see a connection to what John said in chapter 1:

“But to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God, who were born, not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God.” (John 1:12–13, ESV)

Specifically, that our spiritual birth, unlike our physical birth, does not depend on the will of the flesh or the will of man. Back to John 3:5–8:

“Jesus answered, ‘Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born of water and the Spirit he cannot enter into the kingdom of God. That which is born of the flesh is flesh, and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit. Do not be amazed that I said to you, “You must be born again.” The wind blows where it wishes and you hear the sound of it, but do not know where it comes from and where it is going; so is everyone who is born of the Spirit.’” (John 3:5–8, NASB)

It depends solely on God. He initiates it. It is His will that it happens. How is a mystery to us. Just as it is with the wind — where it comes from and where it is going. I should add that there is a slight difference between the two births. We have a choice in our spiritual birth. We did not have one in our physical birth. And that is where we see the beauty of what ‘believe in’ reveals. Our choice. Our response to the Spirit opening our eyes to see Christ. Understanding who He is and what He has done for us. Inviting us to simply open the door when we hear Him knocking on the door of our heart (Revelation 3:20).

I see a few more things about spiritual birth, but we will discuss those in a future post:

  • Being brought into a new family line and nature.
  • The intimate relationship between the begetter and the begotten.
  • The aorist tense of the verb, which means a completed action — for both the physical birth and the spiritual birth.
  • That in the Jewish context, birth was deeply tied to concepts of family lineage and identity. It determined one’s place in society and inheritance rights. The word would evoke thoughts of continuity and belonging within a family or community. Which applies to spiritual birth as well — but much deeper.
