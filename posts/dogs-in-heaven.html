Dogs in Heaven.

This post is mostly for fun. Allowing my imagination to run somewhat unbounded within what I know about heaven. So, bear with me here. I have been thinking a lot about heaven lately. I also have been thinking about dogs lately, and specifically all the dogs I have owned. I love dogs, and I have often told God that I wish I was more like a dog when it comes to my relationship with Him. I will talk more about that in a future post. But as my thinking around both subjects swirled around in my head it was inevitable that they would bump into each other. And they did last night. As I was thinking about heaven on earth, the New Jerusalem, and everything fully restored to what God originally intended (the very thing my God REmakes series is mapping), I had to ask: are the animals also restored? There is no longer a curse on the earth. If I can enjoy everything that I did on earth but now fully in heaven, why not my love for animals?

Let me back up a little. Revelation 21–22 portrays a new heaven and a new earth, God dwelling with humanity, and no more death, mourning, crying, or pain (Revelation 21:1–4). That is clear. But then I was reflecting on Isaiah 65:17–25, which introduces “new heavens and a new earth” — and ends with the wolf and the lamb feeding together and the lion eating straw like the ox (Isaiah 65:25), the famous peaceable-kingdom picture of Isaiah 11:6–9 returning at the finale.

This connection is why I understand Isaiah’s fullest horizon as the renewed creation — often called the New Earth — where God’s peace reaches all of life. But I realize others understand Isaiah 11 as describing a future Messianic reign on the present earth, often called the Millennium, with Revelation 21 describing the still-final state afterward.

AI carefully summarizes the tension in both ideas like this:

Isaiah is certainly promising the peace of God’s kingdom, centered in the Messiah and extending from Israel to the whole earth. The passage may be poetic symbolism of reconciled humanity, but its language also naturally invites hope for a creation in which animals — and people — no longer harm or destroy.

In short: yes, peaceful; yes, connected to God’s restoration of Israel; and yes, many believers take it as pointing ultimately to a renewed earth where even animal violence is gone. The text itself does not settle every timing question, but it unmistakably says that God’s final reign is the opposite of fear, predation, violence, and destruction.

As for me, I am going with it pointing to a renewed earth — Paul even says creation itself will be liberated from decay (Romans 8:21). Right now, I could make a list of everything beautiful that I have experienced in my life that brought joy to me. Of course it was limited, incomplete, broken, and contaminated by sin. Also, the same applied to me, so that my ability to enjoy it was also limited, incomplete, broken and contaminated. Even with both of those restrictions and limitations I knew I was connecting to something that was of God. And it was essentially a preview of what my eternity with Him will be like. Just a shadow. So, it makes more sense to me that if God is making a new earth, and bringing heaven into it, everything will be as I have experienced it, only now it will be perfect. It will be perfect itself, it will be perfect in its environment, and it will be perfect in how I receive it. And it will be that way forever.

Yes, all dogs go to heaven. Yes, all spiders go to heaven also.
