Plato. Theory of Forms. Allegory of the Cave.

I've wondered whether there's a real connection between the spiritual realm and our physical world, beyond the obvious ways Satan and his angels can affect people. We see plenty of that relationship directly in Scripture — Daniel praying and God sending an answer through Gabriel, only for a demon to delay the angel for weeks. Theophanies, dreams, visions, burning bushes, angelic visits. Revelation seems to pull the curtain back further still, though so much of its imagery is clearly meant to communicate things John had no normal vocabulary for. Take Revelation 12:1–9 — almost certainly the birth of Christ and Satan's attempt to kill Him. Is that just imagery God gave John to help him communicate the idea, or is it what was actually happening in the spiritual realm while the Gospels show the same event from the physical side? I lean toward the latter, and wonder what else in Scripture might be a flip side of the same ongoing war — and what we're witnessing in the world right now that might be too.

That question is what pulled me toward Plato's cave — people mistaking shadows on a wall for reality itself. It's a genuinely useful picture for spiritual blindness, and for how badly we need God to open our eyes the way He opened Elisha's servant's eyes in 2 Kings 6, revealing a mountain full of horses and chariots of fire that had been there the whole time. Job's story works the same way — his earthly losses flow directly out of a heavenly council scene we never see happening.

But the analogy only goes so far, and I don't want to overcorrect into something un-biblical. Scripture doesn't treat the physical world as mere illusion the way Plato's cave does. The ultimate “light” isn't an abstract Form of the Good — it's the self-revealing, triune God who entered the physical world in Christ, took on real flesh, died, and rose bodily. The physical isn't something to escape; it's something being redeemed. Ephesians 6:12 is clear that our struggle is “not against flesh and blood, but against spiritual forces of evil,” and that's a real, textured claim — not a license to read every specific tragedy as a one-to-one spiritual transaction. Jesus Himself pushes back on that kind of over-reading in Luke 13.

So: yes, the visible and invisible are genuinely, constantly connected, and the seen often reflects the unseen. But the corrective on Plato is important — the goal isn't escaping the cave into pure abstraction. It's Christ entering the cave Himself, in the flesh, and walking back out again, bodily, on the other side of death.
