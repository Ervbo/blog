The Grammar of Prayer

I set out to answer one question for myself — what does the Bible actually teach about prayer? — and discovered that I had been working with about a quarter of the material my whole life. What follows is the short version. The full study runs to nineteen parts and lives here: The Grammar of Prayer.

Prayer, in the Bible, is not first a technique for obtaining things from God. It is the creature’s answer to a God who has spoken first. Scripture does not introduce it with instructions — it introduces it as something that began: “At that time people began to call on the name of the Lord” (Genesis 4:26). A name had already been given. Prayer is response before it is request. Calvin called it “the chief exercise of faith,” and that is why: faith receives a word, and prayer is faith speaking back.

Its range is far wider than most of us practice. Scripture’s pray-ers adore, confess, give thanks, ask, intercede, listen, lament, vow, bless and cry out — and the vocabulary underneath is correspondingly rich. The Hebrew noun for prayer, tephillah, is built on a root whose reflexive form means to step into the gap. One verse in 1 Timothy stacks four distinct Greek words: supplications, prayers, intercessions, thanksgivings. A prayer life that only asks is using a quarter of the instrument. And a church that never laments has quietly lost a third of the Psalter.

Christian prayer has a definite architecture: to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit (Ephesians 2:18). To pray “in Jesus’ name” is not to add a formula to the end of a sentence but to come on his merit, in union with him, for his stated purposes. And the Son’s mediation is not a workaround for a cold Father — Jesus says plainly, “I do not say to you that I will ask the Father on your behalf; for the Father himself loves you” (John 16:26). Underneath us, the Spirit takes requests we cannot even formulate and conforms them to the will of God. The believer’s prayer is bracketed on both sides by God himself.

Praying together is the default, not the advanced course. The prayer Jesus taught has no singular pronoun in it — our Father, give us, forgive us. Corporate prayer makes that “our” true. It kills indifference by prescription, since Jesus’ remedy for enmity is to pray for the enemy. It levels the room. It distributes weight too heavy for one person to carry. And it builds a shared memory of God that carries a congregation through hard years.

Does it matter how many people pray? Not as leverage — God is not counting signatures, and the Bible’s great answered prayers are startlingly often solo. But Paul asks for many intercessors and tells us exactly why: “so that many will give thanks on our behalf” (2 Corinthians 1:11). God arranges answers so the largest possible number of people have to praise him for them. The chorus afterward is part of the point.

Does it matter how long? When Moses’ arms failed at Rephidim, the answer was more hands, not fewer hours. God posts watchmen on the walls of Jerusalem and tells them to give him no rest (Isaiah 62:6–7). And in Revelation the prayers of the saints are collected in golden bowls and kept — which is the real answer. Nothing you have prayed has evaporated.

Answers come in more forms than yes, no and wait. Granted as asked. Granted in a different currency — the paralytic comes for his legs and is given forgiveness first. Refused with something better named, as with Paul’s thorn. Granted decades later, as with Zechariah, who had surely stopped praying it. Granted through the one who prayed. And, most soberingly, granted in judgment: “He gave them what they asked, but sent a wasting disease among them” (Psalm 106:15). The deepest case is Hebrews 5:7 — Jesus prayed to be spared death, died, and the text says he was heard. Whatever unanswered prayer means, it cannot mean what we usually take it to mean.

What blocks prayer is almost never technique. Scripture names cherished sin, injustice, unforgiveness, dishonor at home, self-indulgent motive, contempt for the poor, idols in the heart — and, most commonly by an enormous margin, simply not asking. What makes prayer effectual is not intensity the pray-er generates. James picks Elijah as his example and immediately says he was “a man with a nature like ours.” The power is in the Son who intercedes above, the Spirit who intercedes within, and the Father who gives good things to those who ask. Effectual prayer is weak prayer laying hold of a strong Christ.

And very often God’s answer is to hand the one who prayed the assignment. Moses prays and is sent. Nehemiah weeps over a ruined city and ends up rebuilding it with his own hands. Jesus tells his disciples to pray for laborers for the harvest — and in the very next sentence sends the men who prayed. Which suggests the right ending to every intercession I make: Lord, am I part of this answer?

The full study takes all of this considerably further — the Hebrew and Greek word study, all fourteen modes, prayer chains and watches, when to stop praying, what Christ’s own prayer life teaches, the hard questions about praying to an unchanging God, a workable daily rule, and an annotated reading list with the classics worth your time.
