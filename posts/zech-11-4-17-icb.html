Biblical March 22, 2026

Zech 11:4-17. ICB.

I stumbled into something this week that I think is worth sharing, and it also gave me a new focus for my quiet time for the rest of the year. Our lesson took us through Zechariah 11:4–17, and I happened to read it in the International Children's Bible (ICB) translation. I then had AI paraphrase the same section in language a child could follow. I realized afterward that I'd read the Bible cover to cover in probably ten different translations over the years, but never once in the ICB — so I'm switching to it for the rest of 2026.

The reason this passage grabbed me is that it echoes the section of Scripture that has affected me more than almost any other: Ezekiel 34, God's indictment of Israel's shepherds who fed themselves instead of the flock. For years I could barely read Ezekiel 34 without ending up in tears — I'd sometimes avoid it altogether. Around the same time, I started thinking differently about Nehemiah, who wept, fasted, and prayed for days over a broken-down wall (Nehemiah 1:3–11), and then risked his life to rebuild it. I saw the same picture in the teenagers around me — walls torn down, exposed, breaking down family by family, church by church, on top of a culture actively working against them. If Nehemiah's heart could break that much over stones, how could mine break any less over kids?

Zechariah 11 is that same shepherd theme turned into a parable, and reading it in two very different registers side by side actually deepened it rather than flattening it.

International Children's Bible (ICB), v.4–17 (abridged)Paraphrased for a child
"Feed the sheep that are about to be killed… Even the shepherds don't feel sorry for their sheep. Even I don't feel sorry anymore for the people of this country," says the Lord. "Take care of my people, like a shepherd takes care of sheep, even though bad things are coming… Even the leaders who should care for the people do not feel sorry for them. So I will stop protecting the people who keep doing wrong."
I took two sticks. I called one Favor. I called the other Union… In one month I got rid of three shepherds. The flock hated me. I took two staffs, like shepherd's sticks. I called one "Favor" and the other "Union"… I got rid of three bad shepherds because I was tired of them and they didn't want me either.
Then I broke the stick named Favor. This broke the agreement God made with his people. Then I took my staff called "Favor" and broke it. This showed that I was ending my special promise to protect all these nations.
So they paid me 30 pieces of silver. The Lord said to me, "Throw the money to the potter." That is how little they thought I was worth. They paid me thirty pieces of silver, which was a small, insulting amount. God said, "Throw that money into the temple. That is how little they think I am worth."
He will not care for the dying sheep. He will not look for the young ones. He will not heal the injured ones… but he will eat the best sheep and tear off their hoofs. How terrible it will be for the useless shepherd. This bad shepherd does not care about the sheep that are dying. He does not look for the lost ones… Trouble will come to this worthless shepherd who leaves the flock. His arm will become weak, and his eye will go blind.

Two things jump out reading it this way. The first is how directly this points forward to Christ — thirty pieces of silver, thrown at a potter, is the exact detail Matthew ties to Judas's betrayal (Matthew 27:3–10). The rejected shepherd of Zechariah 11 becomes the literal price put on the Good Shepherd of John 10. The second is the warning embedded in the last stanza: shepherding isn't a neutral role. A shepherd who won't care for the weak, the lost, and the injured doesn't just fail to help — he actively "tears off their hoofs." Passivity toward the flock curdles into predation.

I'll let you know if reading through the ICB for the rest of the year changes anything for me. But this one section already did what Ezekiel 34 did years ago — it reminded me that God takes bad shepherding personally, and that grief over it is supposed to move us, not just move us to tears.
