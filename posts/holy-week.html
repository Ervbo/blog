Holy Week

Brothers, since I don't know as much as I'd like about the church calendar, I decided to look up the full timeline of Holy Week this week. You all probably know most of this already, but here it is anyway. — Erv

Christians worldwide observe Holy Week every spring, marking the last eight days of Jesus' life on earth. It runs from Palm Sunday to Easter Sunday, and each day carries its own weight:

Palm Sunday — Jesus rides into Jerusalem on a donkey, not a warhorse, and the crowds wave palm branches and hail Him as king (John 12:12–15). Even His choice of transportation was a quiet correction to everyone expecting a political conqueror rather than an eternal one.

Holy Monday — Jesus teaches in the temple, then overturns the money-changers' tables in righteous anger at what the house of worship had become (Matthew 21:12–16). That evening He rests with His friends Lazarus, Mary, and Martha — a quiet moment before the storm builds.

Holy Tuesday — More public teaching, including the parable of the tenants (Matthew 21:33–44), which foreshadows His own coming sacrifice. The religious leaders start looking for a way to arrest Him, but the crowds still love Him too much for them to act.

Holy Wednesday (Spy Wednesday) — Judas approaches the chief priests and agrees to betray Jesus for thirty pieces of silver (John 11:49–53 gives us Caiaphas' chilling logic: better one man die than the nation perish). Meanwhile, Mary anoints Jesus at Bethany with expensive perfume — He calls it preparation for His burial.

Holy Thursday (Maundy Thursday) — “Maundy” comes from the Latin mandatum, “mandate,” referring to Jesus' new command that His followers love one another (John 13:34). This is the night of the Last Supper, the washing of the disciples' feet, and Judas' quiet exit from the table.

Good Friday — Betrayed with a kiss, Jesus is passed from the Jewish authorities to Pilate, beaten, mocked, and crucified. Old Testament prophecies (Psalm 22, Isaiah 53) land with startling precision on the details of that day — the manner of His death, the mockery, the separation from God as He bore the sin of the world.

Holy Saturday — The quietest day of the week. Only Matthew records the detail of the guard posted at the tomb (Matthew 26:62–66), which matters more than it seems — no one could have moved that stone and slipped past a Roman guard undetected.

Easter Sunday — The tomb is empty. An angel meets the women who came to grieve, an earthquake shakes the ground, and Jesus Himself appears to the disciples, His body bearing only the marks of the nails and the spear — proof, not just of survival, but of resurrection.

Brothers, blessed Spy Wednesday to you all — or should I say cursed Spy Wednesday? I'm not big on ritual, but I do love celebrating Christmas and Easter, and not just on the calendar days but always. You've heard me say it before: I've tried to live every day as though Christ died yesterday, rose today, and is coming tomorrow. I need to work “was born” into that sentence somewhere too.

Spy Wednesday, from what I understand, is the day we're meant to pause and remember Judas' betrayal of our Lord. Nothing to celebrate there. Except that I've been like Judas plenty of times myself — denying my Lord one way or another — and like Peter, not Judas, I've crawled back to Him and received His forgiveness. Maybe that's what I can celebrate: that I'm a forgiven Judas on this day. Maybe that's a stretch. Either way, I hope we all take the time to sit — or lay, in my case — before the Lord and remember everything He went through this week, and His whole life really, only to rise in glorious triumph on Sunday.

One more thing while I have you. Dan's email got me thinking — yes, Philip Yancey's Where Is God When It Hurts? is a life-changer. I've read all of Yancey's books. He's a heavy hitter, willing to take on the hardest questions: Where is God when it hurts? Disappointment with God. Does prayer make any difference? What good is God? I'd add The Jesus I Never Knew to Dan's list too.

And since I'm here, a new insight God gave me last night. You all know I'm a Spurgeon fanatic, still working through his 3,000-plus sermons before I die. You also know I've wrestled with this question: can someone truly be saved if they don't believe Jesus is God, or that the Holy Spirit is God? There are reasons people land there — if Jesus were merely an angel, how does an angel's death take away sin? How does an angel become flesh? Without the shedding of blood there's no forgiveness of sin. The verse that's always stood out to me is John 8:24 — “Unless you believe that I am, you will die in your sins.” Look up who Jesus was talking to and what “I AM” meant to them in the Septuagint. It'll blow your mind.

I was rereading Spurgeon's The Personality of the Holy Spirit last night (Sermon #4), and a couple of lines hit me out of nowhere. Spurgeon is saying, plainly, that our baptism is a farce if we don't believe the Holy Spirit is God. BOOM.

I'll admit I've held back from being too dogmatic about this, because if you'd asked me at the moment of my salvation whether Jesus is God, or whether the Holy Spirit is God, I wouldn't have known what you were talking about — and yet I'm certain I was saved in that moment. My life was noticeably different walking home from high school that day, rushing in the door to tell my parents. God holds us accountable for what we know and what He's revealed to us, not more. I read the entire New Testament in about a month after that, and by the end I would have said, yes, Jesus is “probably” God. The Holy Spirit still confused me. It all made sense soon enough. Maybe it's not so different from the thief on the cross — all he knew was that Jesus didn't deserve to die, and that He was going to heaven. “Remember me.”

— Erv

“I shall attempt this morning a little to establish the doctrine and to show you the truth of it — that God the Holy Spirit is actually a Person… Everyone who is baptized according to the true form laid down in Scripture must be a Trinitarian — otherwise his Baptism is a farce and a lie and he himself is found a deceiver and a hypocrite before God.”

“Once give up the doctrine of three Persons and your tackle is all gone… A Gospel without the Trinity? It is a rope of sand that cannot hold together! …But give me a Gospel with the Trinity and the might of Hell cannot prevail against it.”

— Charles Spurgeon, The Personality of the Holy Spirit
