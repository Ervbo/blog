Your Rewards in Heaven. Not Intel.

Part 3 of The Soul series.

When I was at Intel I lived within an intense, fiercely competitive culture — not just with our external competitors like AMD, but internally. Each year every employee had to go through a process called Ranking and Rating. The managers would gather in a room, and each manager would give an account of what each of their employees accomplished and its impact on company objectives. When everyone was finished, they assigned a number to each employee. This number was your Ranking. It was a brutal process, because other managers would fight you for the number you selected if, based on what they had heard, your number was too high — unwarranted. At the end of the session there would be a final list, which would be converted into a Bell Curve. The top ~5% would get a Ranking of Outstanding. The next ~15%, Exceeds Expectations. The next ~60%, Successful. The next ~15%, Below Expectations. The last ~5%, Improvement Required. Raises and stock options were given accordingly. After the Ranking session, the Rating portion began. This was not a forced distribution like the Ranking; rather, the employee’s manager would give a Rating for the pace at which they performed relative to their peers in the same salary grade: Faster Than, Equal, or Slower Than.

I believe this process was inherited from Jack Welch.

Jack Welch, the legendary CEO of General Electric from 1981 to 2001, became controversial for his ruthless focus on short-term profits, massive layoffs, and “stack ranking” of employees — an approach that prioritized soaring stock prices over long-term stability, worker welfare, and industrial health.

On one hand it was very successful. People would work themselves to death to avoid being at the bottom of the distribution, and the resulting overall output for the company was massive. But it came at a cost. Employees would not share knowledge that would help others on their team, or even their organization. They became silos, and would do everything in their power to maintain any competitive advantage they had. And it got even worse than that. Employees would sabotage the work of others — maybe not overtly, but quietly and consistently. Even little things, like casting shade on someone’s work, or doubting and criticizing them in front of everyone. Calling them out unnecessarily in group emails. That was the worst:

I don’t like you or what you did — and rather than calling you or emailing you to discuss it, I created a team email, copied your manager, and condemned you for all to see.

I went through that process for five or six years, and then one day it dawned on me. Yes, the Ranking and Rating process worked perfectly at producing the maximum output of the individual employees. But it worked against the maximum output of the team. It was destroying the team on an installment plan.

So I came up with an idea. Why not give two raises? Do two sessions. The first would remain the same: how did the employee do? What did they contribute? The second would be similar, but would ask — how did the employee affect the team? What did they do to make the team better? In this way there is a counterbalance, and you get the best of both worlds. One does not need to excel at the expense of the other. I thought it was brilliant. I still do. I submitted it to HR and never received a response.

What does any of this have to do with God, and with our soul? It is this. I noticed over my last couple of posts that we get to decide where our soul spends eternity. And we also get to decide, daily as we live, what the condition of our soul will be in eternity. Rewards, prosperity, stewardship… all from building God’s kingdom rather than our own. Storing up treasures — in our soul — in heaven.

But what about what we did for the Body of Christ? With the Body of Christ? Can our souls also be expanded and made fruitful by what we did in our church? Or Bible study? Or whatever ministry God put us in? Just like my example above: Intel could have rewarded employees for what they did individually and as a team. They chose not to, for the team. But God will. His Word is clear about that.
