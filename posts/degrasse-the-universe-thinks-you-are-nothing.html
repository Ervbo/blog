deGrasse. The universe thinks you are nothing.

Before I get into this, take a minute with Psalm 8 — it sets the stage for everything that follows:

When I consider Your heavens, the work of Your fingers, the moon and the stars, which You have ordained; what is man that You take thought of him, and the son of man that You care for him? Yet You have made him a little lower than God, and You crown him with glory and majesty!

— Psalm 8:3–6

I was watching a Neil deGrasse Tyson lecture on cosmology — he's not one of my favorite lecturers, because as brilliant as he is with the data and the equations of the universe, he consistently fails in his conclusions. This video, roughly thirty-six minutes, is a prime example (watch it here, or read the transcript).

His opening line: “The universe thinks you're nothing. Literally, nothing. And I can prove it mathematically.” He walks through the math — if the observable universe were examined through a microscope for the first time, humanity would be so far below the threshold of detection we might as well not exist. He extends it to time as well: compress the universe's 13.8-billion-year history into a single calendar year, and human civilization shows up in the last few seconds of December 31st. Your entire life would be measured in microseconds. Cosmically small, and cosmically brief.

Here's the part that got me, though — Tyson himself doesn't stop there. He goes on to argue that despite all that, you're also the most remarkable thing the universe has produced: 13.8 billion years of atoms following physical laws with no awareness of themselves, until, through evolution, those same atoms arranged into something complex enough to contemplate its own existence. You are made of star stuff, literally — the carbon in your muscles and the iron in your blood were forged in the nuclear furnace of stars that lived and died before our solar system existed. He calls it a paradox: simultaneously the most insignificant and the most significant thing in the known universe. He then walks through everything that had to go right — the exact expansion rate after the Big Bang, generations of stellar death and rebirth to produce heavy elements, a solar system in just the right galactic location, Earth at just the right distance from the sun — as a case for staggering cosmic improbability.

Here's where I part ways with him entirely. He takes all of that improbability and assigns it to time and chance, and somehow derives meaning from that. And Christians get accused of blind faith? For me it's the exact opposite reaction. I agree completely that on a cosmic scale I'm insignificant — in my own city I'm insignificant. It's a miracle I'm even here, that I have a life, consciousness, a soul. But set that insignificance next to how much God loves me — that Christ came after me, suffered for me, died for me — and the contrast is overwhelming. I'm a piece of cosmic dust that has no reason to exist, and yet I'm valued by God to a degree I can't begin to comprehend. That contrast should blow anyone's mind.

Read Psalm 8 again with all of that in view. It reads differently once you've sat with the actual scale of the universe for a while.
