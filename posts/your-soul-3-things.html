Your Soul. The 3 Things.

In The Soul I made the case that, as an eternal living soul, you really only have two decisions to make in your entire life: 1) Where do I want my soul to spend eternity? 2) In what condition do I want my soul to be in eternity? I took that framework back into my study library and asked what, if anything, I might be missing. Here is the response — and it adds a third thing.

The response:

Your framework captures something essential, but the sources suggest a third dimension worth considering: the nature of your motives and the quality of your work itself.

Your two decisions are foundational and biblically sound. Eternal life comes through faith alone in Christ alone, while believers gain or lose eternal rewards based on their personal faithfulness.[1] The first decision determines your eternal destination; the second shapes your eternal condition through the rewards you accumulate.

However, what affects your eternal soul extends beyond these two choices into how God evaluates the substance of your service. Some believers suffer loss of reward because their post-salvation works were unrewardable, because sin and selfishness corrupted their motives, or because outright disobedience caused God’s chastening.[2] This suggests God examines not just whether you served His kingdom, but how and why you served it.

The judgment involves penetrating assessment. God will bring your darkest secrets to light and reveal your private motives, then give to each one whatever praise is due (1 Cor 4:5). On judgment day, fire will reveal what kind of work each builder has done and show if a person’s work has any value (1 Cor 3:10–15) — meaning some service, even if done for God’s kingdom, may not survive this scrutiny.

Additionally, the duration of reward or loss of reward is eternal,[3] making the consequences of your ongoing choices profound and permanent. Your second decision — what condition you want your soul in — compounds across eternity based on the integrity of your choices, not merely their direction.

So, while your framework is biblically grounded, add this: the authenticity of your motives and the quality of your obedience matter eternally, not just the kingdom you’re building for.

— end of the response. So the framework grows by one. Where will my soul spend eternity? In what condition will it be? And now the third thing, the one that searches deepest: with what motives am I building? The first decision is settled once. The second never stops. The third runs underneath the second every single day — not just whose kingdom, but why. Note to self, part two.

[1] Fred R. Lybrand, “Corporate Rewards: Does the Church You Attend Matter to God?” Chafer Theological Seminary Journal Vol. 11 (2005).
[2] Rolland McCune, A Systematic Theology of Biblical Christianity: The Doctrines of Salvation, the Church, and Last Things (Detroit Baptist Theological Seminary, 2010), 416.
[3] Douglas C. Bozung, “Degrees of Rewards in Eternity: Sanctification by Works?” Journal of the Grace Evangelical Society Vol. 24 (2011).
